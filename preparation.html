Test preparation

How to prepare for your laboratory test

Preparation is different for every test. Some need fasting, many don't, and a few have very specific instructions. Getting it right helps make your result accurate and can save you a repeat visit.

Your own instructions come first. If your healthcare provider or laboratory has given you instructions, follow them, even if they differ from the general guidance here.

Never stop or change a prescribed medicine before a test unless your healthcare provider tells you to.

Before any test

  • Check your request form or appointment letter for any preparation instructions.
  • Bring your request form, if you were given one, and any identification your clinic asks for.
  • Have a list of all the medicines, herbal remedies and supplements you take.
  • Unless you've been told otherwise, drink water as normal. Being well hydrated makes blood collection easier.
  • Wear clothing with sleeves that roll up easily.
  • Tell the staff if you feel anxious about needles, or have fainted during blood tests before. They can help you lie down.

Fasting

Most laboratory tests do not need fasting. It is only needed for tests where a recent meal could change the result, such as a fasting glucose test.

What fasting means

Fasting means having nothing to eat or drink, except plain water, for a set number of hours before your sample is taken. The usual time is between 8 and 12 hours, but it depends on the test. Your healthcare provider or laboratory will tell you how long.

Can I drink water?

Yes. Plain water is almost always allowed while fasting, and it helps keep you hydrated. If you are told otherwise for a specific test, follow that instruction.

What to avoid while fasting

  • Food of any kind, including snacks and sweets
  • Tea and coffee, even without milk or sugar
  • Juice, soft drinks, energy drinks and alcohol
  • Chewing gum and mints
  • Smoking and strenuous exercise, which can also affect some results

Don't fast for longer than you need to

Fasting for much longer than instructed can change some results. Book a morning appointment if you can, so most of your fast happens overnight, and bring something to eat afterwards.

If you have diabetes

Fasting can cause your blood sugar to drop too low if you take insulin or some other diabetes medicines. Ask your healthcare provider in advance how to manage your medicines on the day. If you feel shaky, sweaty, confused or faint, eat or drink something sugary and tell the staff. The test can be rearranged.

When samples are collected

For many tests, the time of day doesn't matter. For some, it does:

  • Hormones that follow a daily rhythm, such as cortisol, are often measured at a set time, usually in the morning.
  • Medicine levels are often measured at a set time in relation to your last dose, for example just before the next one is due.
  • Monitoring tests, such as an INR for warfarin, are best done at a similar time on each occasion.
  • Urine tests may ask for the first urine of the morning, which is more concentrated.

If your form gives a time, try to stick to it, and tell the staff if your sample is collected at a different time.

Medicines and supplements

Keep taking your prescribed medicines as normal unless your healthcare provider has told you otherwise. Stopping a medicine, even for a day, can sometimes be harmful.

Some medicines and supplements can affect test results. That doesn't usually mean you should stop them. It means the person interpreting your result needs to know you take them. Always tell your healthcare provider about:

  • prescribed medicines and anything you buy without a prescription
  • herbal and traditional remedies
  • vitamins and supplements, especially biotin (vitamin B7), which is found in many hair, skin and nail products and can interfere with some hormone tests

For some tests, your healthcare provider may ask you to take a dose at a particular time, for example after your sample rather than before. Follow their advice.

Blood samples

Most blood samples are taken from a vein on the inside of your elbow and take only a few minutes. You can read what happens when blood is collected in our Learn section.

Tips for a smoother blood test

  • Drink water beforehand (unless told not to). It makes veins easier to find.
  • Keep warm. Cold hands and arms can make veins harder to find.
  • Tell the staff about any bleeding problems or blood-thinning medicines.
  • Afterwards, press firmly on the area for a few minutes to reduce bruising.

Urine samples

Midstream urine sample

Used for most urine tests, including tests for infection. Collecting the middle part of the stream reduces contamination from bacteria on the skin.

  1. Use the sterile container you were given. Don't touch the inside of it or its lid.
  2. Wash your hands, and clean around your genitals with water.
  3. Start passing urine into the toilet, then, without stopping the flow, collect the middle part in the container.
  4. Finish passing urine into the toilet, close the lid firmly and label the container.
  5. Return it as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours, or ask whether to keep it in the fridge.

See urine microscopy, culture and sensitivity for more detail.

24-hour urine collection

Some tests measure everything your kidneys pass over a full day. You will be given a large container, and sometimes written instructions specific to the test.

  1. On the first morning, pass urine into the toilet as normal and do not collect it. Write down the date and time. This is your start time.
  2. Collect all the urine you pass for the next 24 hours in the container.
  3. At the same time the next morning, pass urine one last time and add it to the container. This is the end of the collection.
  4. Keep the container cool, as instructed, and return it promptly.

Some containers contain a small amount of preservative, which may be an acid. Don't empty it out, avoid contact with your skin, and keep the container away from children.

Stool samples

Stool (faeces) samples are used to look for infections, blood or other substances. Some home screening kits come with their own instructions; always follow those.

  1. Pass urine first if you need to, so it doesn't mix with the stool sample.
  2. Catch the stool before it touches the toilet water, for example using a clean, disposable container or a sheet of paper placed over the toilet bowl.
  3. Use the scoop attached to the lid of your container to collect a small amount. Fill only to the line, if there is one.
  4. Close the lid firmly, label the container, and wash your hands well.
  5. Return the sample as soon as you can. Some tests need it within a few hours.

Tell your healthcare provider if you have recently taken antibiotics or travelled abroad, as this helps the laboratory decide which tests to do.
