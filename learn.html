Why do I need to fast before some blood tests?

When you eat, your body absorbs sugars and fats from the food into your blood. For a few hours afterwards, the levels of some substances, especially glucose and triglycerides, are higher than usual.

For tests where this matters, fasting gives a steady “baseline” level that can be compared fairly with reference intervals and with your earlier results. For example, a fasting plasma glucose test is interpreted on the basis that you haven't eaten.

Most tests don't need fasting at all, and some that used to, such as a routine lipid profile, often no longer do. Plain water is almost always allowed. Your request form or healthcare provider will tell you if you need to fast. See our guide to fasting.

What happens when blood is collected?

Having blood taken is called venepuncture, or phlebotomy. It usually takes just a few minutes.

  1. The healthcare worker checks your identity, usually your full name and date of birth, and confirms which tests are needed. This makes sure your sample is matched to you.
  2. You sit or lie down, and a band (tourniquet) is placed around your upper arm to make the veins easier to see and feel.
  3. The skin is cleaned and a small, sterile needle is inserted into a vein, usually on the inside of your elbow. You may feel a sharp scratch.
  4. Blood flows into one or more tubes. Different tubes contain different additives, depending on the tests requested.
  5. The needle is removed and you are asked to press on the area for a few minutes. A small plaster or cotton wool is put on.
  6. Your tubes are labelled and sent to the laboratory.

A small bruise is common and fades within a few days. If you feel faint or anxious, tell the staff. You can lie down, and it's very common to feel this way.

What is a reference range?

A reference range, also called a reference interval, is the range of results a laboratory usually finds in healthy people. It is printed next to your result so it can be compared.

It is usually based on the middle 95% of results from a healthy group. This means about 1 in 20 healthy people will have a result just outside the range for any single test. A reference range is a guide, not a line between “healthy” and “unwell”.

Reference ranges can differ with age, sex and pregnancy, and between laboratories. Read more about reference intervals.

What does an abnormal result mean?

An “abnormal” result is one that falls outside the reference interval. It does not necessarily mean that something is wrong.

A result can be outside the interval because of normal variation, what you ate or drank, exercise, medicines, a recent mild illness, or how the sample was handled. Many slightly abnormal results go back to normal when repeated.

On the other hand, some important changes can happen while a result is still within the interval, which is why your healthcare provider may compare your result with your previous ones.

Your healthcare provider will interpret your result alongside your symptoms, history and other tests, and explain whether anything further is needed. If you are worried about a result, ask them.

Why can two laboratories report slightly different results?

If the same sample were tested in two laboratories, the results would often be very close, but not always identical. This is because laboratories may:

  • use different analysers and reagents, which are calibrated slightly differently
  • use different methods to measure the same substance
  • report in different units

Every measurement also has a small amount of natural variation, called measurement uncertainty. Laboratories monitor this closely with daily quality control checks and external quality assessment schemes.

This is why each laboratory provides its own reference intervals, and why it is best, where possible, to have repeat tests at the same laboratory.

Why do laboratories sometimes ask for a repeat sample?

Being asked for another sample can be worrying, but it is common and often routine. Reasons include:

  • The first sample couldn't be tested, for example because it was damaged or delayed (see below).
  • To confirm an unexpected result before any decisions are made. Some results, such as potassium, are easily affected by how the sample was handled.
  • To see whether a change persists. Many small abnormalities settle on their own.
  • Because guidelines require it. Some conditions can only be diagnosed after more than one test.
  • To monitor treatment, for example after a medicine dose has been changed.

Why was my sample rejected?

Laboratories reject samples that could give an unreliable or unsafe result. A rejected sample is rarely anything you did wrong. Common reasons include:

  • Labelling problems. Missing or mismatched details mean the laboratory can't be sure the sample is yours. For safety, these are not tested.
  • Haemolysis. Red blood cells were damaged, releasing their contents into the sample. This can falsely change results such as potassium.
  • Clotted sample. Some tests need blood that hasn't clotted.
  • Wrong tube or container, or a tube filled with too little blood. Some tubes, such as those for clotting tests, must be filled to an exact line.
  • Delay. Some substances change if the sample takes too long to reach the laboratory.
  • Leaking or contaminated containers.

Rejecting a sample protects you from receiving a misleading result. If you are asked to give another sample, it's because the laboratory wants your result to be accurate.
